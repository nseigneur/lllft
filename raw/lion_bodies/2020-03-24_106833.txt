TITLE: Ntsevu Lioness Mates With Othawa Male
DATE: 2020-03-24
URL: https://blog.londolozi.com/2020/03/24/ntsevu-lioness-mates-with-othawa-male/

The big question that we all have is could one of the Ntsevu Pride lionesses be infertile? Five of the six lionesses in the pride have had cubs over the last two years, but one is still yet to give birth. The remaining lioness has been seen mating over and over again with the Birmingham males, but to no avail. The same lioness a couple of months ago seen being followed by one of the Birmingham males. The present and the future. Cubs from the Ntsevu Pride look on as one of their fathers – a Birmingham male – look towards them in the background. Lionesses will typically mate with several males within an area to ensure that each male believes the cubs to be his own. In this way, the cubs gain protection from all the males in a territory and avoid infanticide. It is also known that not every oestrus cycle will result in ovulation. This may be a strategy to ensure that when ovulation does occur, it is likely that the strongest coalition of males will have settled in a territory. In this manner, by the time a lioness conceives, cubs are protected by the presence of territorial males holding the strongest genes in that area at that time. Two of the Birmingham male lions lie in close proximity. These males currently defend a territory that spans across most of the southern and eastern part of Londolozi. Younger males have ventured through briefly, but none have yet challenged the coalition for ownership of the area and the lionesses within. About three weeks ago, a single Ntsevu lioness was found mating with one of the Birmingham males. They were together for about four days, mating consistently – as lions do. The lioness in question yawns as a Birmingham male lifts his nose in the Flehmen grimace. This is when a male appears to snarl, but is in fact analysing a scent. In this case he was analysing the pheromones being released by the lioness in oestrus. They proceeded to mate shortly after this. Then, only a few days ago, a mating pair was found in the far southwest of Londolozi. We would expect to find either the Mungheni pride or even the Southern pride in these parts. The male was easy enough to identify as the Othawa male. Upon close inspection of the lioness’ whisker pattern, it was confirmed to be the same Ntsevu lioness as pictured above. The Othawa male jumps away from the lioness as he extracts from copulation. He was avoiding any potential flying claws from the lioness. In this case, she only rolled over and did not strike out at him, which quite often happens. A clear view of the potentially infertile Ntsevu lioness moments after mating with the Othawa male. The pair was viewed mating for roughly three days. We hypothesise that one of the Birmingham males wondered into the area, roaring, which may have caused the Othawa male to up and move west into more familiar territory. This is based on tracks that were followed in the area, leading straight to where a Birmingham male was found. The lioness was miles away from the safety of the rest of her pride, but clearly that’s what instinct can drive a lioness to do. Will these recent matings finally result in this lioness falling pregnant? Only time will tell, but we think not…
